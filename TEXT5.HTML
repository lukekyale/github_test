DEVON TURNBULL

HIFI LISTENING ROOM DREAM NO. 1

2023

BB: HiFi Listening Room Dream No. 1 is the culmination of a lifetime of passion and experience. It’s an experience to be celebrated and absorbed within.

DT: The best way for the piece to exist is for it to be completely democratic and available to everyone for free. Hi-fi can normally feel gated or inaccessible, so the listening room is meant to remove that pressure and allow anyone who is curious to simply come in and spend time.

When people feel no pressure about how they are supposed to listen, they can have a much deeper emotional experience. Sometimes people even lie down and fall asleep in the space.

BB: You’ve dismantled some of the snobbery around something that was previously inaccessible. Why is creating that environment around music so important?

DT: When listening to music, set and setting are extremely important. That idea led me toward building my own hi-fi equipment and understanding the entire signal chain so I could develop a closer relationship with music.

I wanted to create a kind of shrine for music in my home, a dedicated space where listening itself becomes the primary activity. We have access to almost every piece of music imaginable now, but that accessibility can also turn music into something that simply exists in the background.

BB: What are the foundations of setting up a good sound system, regardless of someone's means?

DT: I try not to be too dogmatic about what makes a good sound system. The intention is more important.

Personally, I like efficient speakers and minimalist, low-power amplifiers because they can create an emotional, visceral, and textural sound. But there are many different philosophies for designing speakers and sound systems.

The best way to learn is to listen to as many different systems as possible. Find something that creates an emotional connection for you, study it, understand how it works, and eventually find ways of building your own creation from what you have learned.

BB: So much of your work is about context. Talk to me about music in its environment and how that becomes part of your vision.

DT: Light and air are important aspects of listening to music. I even make smells specifically for listening. When people enter the listening room, they often instinctively whisper, sit down, and close their eyes.

The physical presence of the system matters too. When music becomes completely invisible, such as playing a file from the internet without any physical anchor, it can easily become background music.

That led to the idea of the music shrine. The more my own hand goes into crafting the system and shaping its sound, the more directly connected I feel to the performance and the musicians who created it.

BB: How does the playback system affect our relationship with the original musical performance?

DT: Recorded music forms a kind of closed loop. A musician creates acoustic vibrations, a microphone transforms those vibrations into electronic information, they are recorded and stored, and eventually a speaker turns that information back into vibrations in the air.

The artist and recording engineer control one part of that process, but the listener and their playback system control the other. Because of that, you are directly involved in the music you are listening to whether you realize it or not.

Bringing intention to that process makes listening feel more connected to the original performance.

BB: 180 Studios has acquired the work. Do you hope it can continue functioning as a public listening space?

DT: The intention is for the work to remain extremely easy to access. People should be able to walk in from the street and experience it without needing special knowledge about hi-fi or music.

There should be zero gatekeeping. Whether someone is a famous musician or simply a tourist who heard that something interesting was happening, everyone should be equally welcome to sit down and experience the system.

BB: Listening bars from Tokyo have increasingly influenced spaces in the US and UK, but something about the original culture doesn't always translate.

DT: The desire to create spaces specifically for appreciating music in a more considerate way is a good thing. Places such as listening bars attempt to create environments where music is treated differently from ordinary background sound.

But there is a cultural difference. In many Western spaces, people naturally expect a bar to be a place for socializing. In some Japanese listening bars, the expectation is completely different: listening itself is the activity.
